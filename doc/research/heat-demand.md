# Heat demand: real figures for the LTHW ring and its consumers

Researched 2026-11-01 for mission 120 (`doc/prompts/120-heat-demand-lthw-ring.md`), which models the boiler house's low temperature hot water (LTHW) ring and every facility drawing heat from it. The owner: "everything is real … if we're faking heat demand, which is one of the most important game elements, we're basically cheating." This note gives the real-world figures the model should use, with their sources, and checks the game's existing figures against them. It is research only: no code is changed by it. It builds on `doc/research/boiler-house-control.md` (how the boilers are sequenced) and does not repeat it.

The setting: a UK leisure centre of the 2000s with a 50 m Olympic pool (2,500 m³, 1,250 m²), a leisure pool (the Lounge, 138.9 m³, 153.8 m²), a jacuzzi (2.83 m³, 4.52 m²) and a footbath (0.9 m³, 6 m²), a museum of 3,038 m² heated from the same boiler house, and a reception and changing area not built yet.

## 1. What the game models today

The figures below were worked out from the code at `c3925d55` (`thermalParams` run on each pool's sizing, `areaParams` on `AREAS`).

Each pool and its hall is a two-body model (`src/game/plant/thermal.ts`): the water, heated by its exchanger and losing heat by evaporation, convection to the hall air and conduction to the ground; and the hall air with its fabric (ten times the air's heat capacity), heated by its air handling units' coils and losing heat through its envelope (U 0.35 W/m²K) and by infiltration (0.5 air changes an hour). Each heater's output is `headroom` (2.5) times its design load, and the design loads are worked out at `AMBIENT`, 12 °C. Its conductance from the ring is `k = output / (80 − T_secondary)` (`waterK`, `airK`).

The halls' loss conductances (fabric plus infiltration): the Olympic 10.3 kW/K (3.06 fabric, 7.21 infiltration of a 43,000 m³ hall), the Lounge 1.28 kW/K, the jacuzzi room 0.29 kW/K, the footbath passage 0.071 kW/K.

The other areas (`src/game/plant/heat-consumers.ts`): the museum at 50 W/m² of floor at −3 °C outside and 20 °C inside, 151.9 kW design, heaters 1.2× (182 kW, k 3.04 kW/K), a mass of 100 kJ/K per m² of floor (304 MJ/K, UA 6.6 kW/K); reception, changing and DHW with no load. The ring (`src/game/plant/lthw-ring.ts`): 80 °C set point, 5 m³ of water, 80 m³/h, losses 0.3 kW/K, six boilers of 300 kW (the Hamwarmy HW 300, `condensingBoilerModel`), 1,800 kW in all.

Note that three different "outside" temperatures are in use: `AMBIENT` 12 °C in `thermal.ts` (the pools' design loads), `OUTSIDE` 5 °C in `heat-loss.ts`, and `DESIGN_OUTSIDE` −3 °C in `heat-consumers.ts` (the museum). 12 °C is close to the UK's annual mean, so the pools' "design loads" are really their mean loads, and their 2.5× headroom is what carries them through a winter design day. This matters for the checks in section 9.

## 2. The LTHW primary

**Flow and return.** UK LTHW systems were traditionally designed for an 11 K difference, 82 °C flow and 71 °C return, with non-condensing boilers; for condensing boilers designers moved to a 20 K difference, 80/60 °C or 85/65 °C, and lower still (70/50 °C, or 70/40 °C) where the emitters allow it, because a gas boiler only condenses with its return below about 55 °C, the dew point of its flue gas (Modbs, "New boilers for old radiators"; Logic4Training; Building, "Heat treatment"). Many older systems ran at a constant 80/60 °C. A pool plant is a special case: the pools and the DHW want hot water whatever the weather, so the primary runs at a fixed set point, about 80 °C, with any weather-compensated radiator circuits on mixing valves downstream (`boiler-house-control.md` §2.2). Sport England's schematic for a typical small pool shows exactly this: boilers on a low loss header, a pool calorifier, the pool hall's air handling unit's heater battery, and radiators on a weather-compensated valve (Sport England 2013, Appendix 2, p. 6).

For a centre of the 2000s with condensing boilers, the normal choice is **80 °C flow, 60 °C return at design (ΔT 20 K)**. At part load, with a constant-flow primary, the return rises towards the flow (the classic "low ΔT" of a constant-volume ring), and the boilers condense less: a real effect worth keeping.

**Ring flow per kW.** The flow follows from the duty and ΔT: `flow (m³/h) = Q (kW) / (1.163 × ΔT)`. At 20 K that is 0.043 m³/h per kW (12 l/s per MW); at 11 K (82/71) 0.078 m³/h per kW. For the game's 1,800 kW at 20 K: 77 m³/h, so the game's 80 m³/h is right. In DN150 steel (ID about 154 mm) that is about 1.2 m/s, a normal velocity.

**Water volume (thermal mass).** The rule of thumb for a whole heating system's water content, used to size expansion vessels and pressurisation units, is about 10 to 12 litres per kW of boiler output (a pressurisation maker's chart assumes 10 l/kW; the 12 l/kW figure is attributed to BS 7074-1; domestic systems about 6 l/kW). That figure is dominated by radiators. A pool plant's primary ring feeds mostly plate heat exchangers, calorifier coils and AHU batteries, which hold little water, so its own volume is much lower. A bottom-up estimate for the game: six modular condensing boilers at about 30 to 60 l each (0.2 to 0.4 m³), a low loss header (about 0.3 to 0.5 m³), about 300 m of DN100 to DN150 steel (8.2 l/m and 18.6 l/m: about 3 to 4 m³), and the exchangers' and batteries' primaries (about 0.5 to 1 m³): **about 4.5 to 6 m³, or 2.5 to 3.3 l/kW**. If the museum's radiator circuits were counted in the ring they would add about 1 to 2 m³ (panel radiators hold about 5 to 8 l per kW of output), but they are better counted in the museum's own mass. The game's 5 m³ is right. Its time constant: 5 m³ × 4,190 kJ/m³K = 20,950 kJ/K, so with the gas cut and 700 kW still drawn the ring falls about 2 K a minute, 80 to 50 °C in about a quarter of an hour.

**Distribution losses.** Insulated LTHW pipework to BS 5422 loses roughly 10 to 35 W per metre between DN25 and DN150 at 75 °C (the standard's tables for non-domestic heating at 75 °C and 15 °C still air; the figures here are approximate and were not checked against the current 2023 edition), so 300 m of mostly large pipe loses about 6 to 9 kW; uninsulated valves and flanges, the boilers' casings and the low loss header add as much again. A compact, well-insulated plant loses about 1 to 3 % of its annual heat in distribution; long, poorly lagged runs 5 to 15 %. The Carbon Trust notes that insulating boilers, tanks, pipes and valves pays back "within a few months" (CTG009, p. 18). The game's 0.3 kW/K, about 18 kW at 80 °C against a 20 °C room, is about 2.5 % of a 700 kW mean load: right for a plant of the period kept in good order.

## 3. Pool water heating

**Design loads.** The water's load is evaporation first, then convection to the air, conduction to the ground, and heating the make-up and backwash replacement. The standard evaporation formula (ASHRAE Handbook, HVAC Applications, as EnergyPlus implements it) is `W = 0.1 × A × AF × (p_w − p_a)` in IP units, which is **0.144 kg/h per m² per kPa** of vapour-pressure difference in SI, with an activity factor AF of 0.5 for a recreational or elderly-swimmers pool, 0.65 for competition, diving and fitness, 0.8 for a hotel, 1.0 for a public or school pool and for a spa, and 1.5 to 2.0 for wave pools and flumes. For the Olympic at 28 °C water, 29 °C air and 60 % RH the difference is 1.375 kPa, so 248 kg/h at AF 1.0 and 161 kg/h at AF 0.65. The game's 162 kg/h is exactly the competition figure. Its latent heat is about 110 kW, **88 W/m²** of water; with convection and conduction the water's load is about 94 W/m² at a 12 °C mean and about 100 to 105 W/m² on a winter design day. At AF 1.0 (a busy public session) it would be about 140 W/m². For the Lounge (30/31 °C) the game's 32 kg/h corresponds to AF 0.93, near a public pool's 1.0, giving about 150 W/m²: right for a leisure pool with more splashing and less swimming. Spas evaporate far more when their jets and air blowers run (AF 1.0 still, but with aerated, agitated water the real figure is often double); the game's jacuzzi figure is quiet-water.

So for an indoor pool held 1 °C under its hall air at 60 % RH: **about 90 to 150 W/m² of water surface**, the low end for a lane pool, the high end for a leisure pool in use. The Olympic's design water load is about 120 to 130 kW, the Lounge's about 25 kW.

**Heater sizing.** The Carbon Trust: the pool heater "is typically sized, based on raising the pool water temperature by 0.5 °C per hour (to adjust temperature and 'make good' heat lost by, for example, evaporation and backwashing). If a pool is being heated from cold, the rate must be no more than 0.25 °C per hour, otherwise the expansion of materials may cause problems to the pool structure or lining" (CTG009, p. 14). Water's heat is 1.163 kWh per m³ per K, so:

| Pool              | kWh per K | 0.25 K/h | 0.5 K/h  | Game's heater now | Game's net warm-up rate at design |
| ----------------- | --------- | -------- | -------- | ----------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Olympic, 2,500 m³ | 2,908     | 727 kW   | 1,454 kW | 293 kW            | 0.06 K/h (about 1.4 K/day)        |
| Lounge, 138.9 m³  | 162       | 40 kW    | 81 kW    | 57 kW             | 0.21 K/h                          |
| Jacuzzi, 2.83 m³  | 3.3       | 0.8 kW   | 1.6 kW   | 4.2 kW            | 0.76 K/h                          |
| Footbath, 0.9 m³  | 1.05      | 0.26 kW  | 0.5 kW   | 2.5 kW            | 1.4 K/h                           |

For a 2,500 m³ pool the 0.5 K/h rule gives an exchanger of nearly 1.5 MW, which few designers would fit; normal practice for a large pool is to size for a heat-up from a cold fill in about three to four days, which is the 0.25 K/h limit: about 730 kW, plus the losses that grow as it warms, **about 750 to 850 kW**. Packaged pool exchanger makers size small pools on the same basis: Stokvis's Econoplate pool tables are for a 72-hour heat-up (for example about 9.8 kW for 30 m³ through a 20 K rise), and Bowman publishes its tube-type pool exchangers' outputs at 82 °C primary water (for example its FG100 at 90 to 170 kW for primary flows of 30 to 100 l/min, for pools up to 250 m³). The game's Olympic heater, at 293 kW, would take nine or ten days to warm a fresh fill from 10 °C: slow by normal practice, though within the structural limit. Spas are sized to reheat after a drain-down within a shift (a jacuzzi from 10 to 37 °C in about eight hours needs about 11 to 12 kW).

**k (UA) and approach.** A real plate heat exchanger transfers `Q = UA × LMTD`. At the design duty, primary 80 in and 60 out, pool water 27 in and 28 out (the Olympic's 620 m³/h through a 750 kW exchanger rises about 1 K): LMTD = (52 − 33) / ln(52/33) ≈ 42 K, so UA ≈ 18 kW/K; the cold-end approach (primary return to pool inlet) is about 33 K and the hot-end 52 K. The game's simpler `k = Q / (T_flow − T_pool)` is then 750 / 52 ≈ 14.4 kW/K. A condensing-friendly selection for 70/50 °C primary would have an LMTD of about 32 K and needs about a third more surface. A gasketed plate exchanger's overall U in clean water service is roughly 3 to 6 kW/m²K, so 18 kW/K is about 3 to 6 m² of plates: a small exchanger for its duty, as pool exchangers are.

**How fast a pool cools with no heat.** At its design loss and no heat, a pool cools at `load / (1.163 × volume)` K/h. The Olympic: 117 kW / 2,908 kWh/K ≈ 0.04 K/h, **about 1 K a day** (the comment in `thermal.ts` says the same); with a pool cover on overnight much less (covers save 10 to 30 % of total pool energy, CTG009 p. 15). The Lounge about 0.14 K/h (3 to 4 K a day), the jacuzzi about 0.5 K/h quiet and 1 to 2 K/h with its jets running, the footbath about 1 K/h. As the water cools its evaporation falls, so the rate slows towards the hall air's temperature. These are the right orders: big pools lose a degree a day, spas degrees an hour.

**Water temperatures.** PWTAG (2009, quoted by Sport England 2013, Appendix 2, Table 4): competitive swimming and diving 28 °C maximum, conventional main pool 29 °C, leisure pools 30 °C, children 31 °C, babies and disabled 32 °C, hydrotherapy 35 °C, spa pools 40 °C. The game's Olympic 28 °C, Lounge 30 °C and jacuzzi 37 °C match; the footbath at 34 °C is a design choice with no PWTAG figure.

## 4. Pool hall air heating

**Conditions.** Sport England: hall air "usually kept at one degree above the pool water temperature to minimise evaporation", about 30 °C for 29 °C water; relative humidity 60 % ± 10 %; air velocity about 0.1 m/s in occupied areas; minimum fresh air supply 4 to 10 air changes an hour; spectator areas about 25 °C with 10 air changes; changing areas 24 to 25 °C with 10 air changes, on a separate system; ancillary areas 20 °C (Sport England 2013, Appendix 2, p. 7). The Carbon Trust: a guideline of **10 litres of ventilation air per second per m² of total pool hall area** (water plus wet surrounds), normally about 4 to 6 air changes an hour depending on the hall's height; 8 to 10 with extensive water features (CTG009, p. 10). The game's halls are 1 °C over their water (Olympic 29/28, Lounge 31/30), matching.

**Airflow for the game's halls.** The Olympic hall, 66 × 40 m (2,640 m²), at 10 l/s/m²: 26.4 m³/s (95,000 m³/h), which in its 43,000 m³ is 2.2 air changes an hour, low because of its tall roof; at Sport England's minimum 4 air changes, 48 m³/s. A 50 m pool hall would have two to four large AHUs of 10 to 20 m³/s each. The Lounge, 28 × 20 m: 5.6 m³/s, 5.1 air changes in its 3,920 m³.

**Supply air temperature.** In heating, the supply air is delivered a few degrees above the hall, typically up to 35 to 40 °C at low level along the glazing, to offset the fabric loss and wash the windows against condensation (Sport England 2013, Appendix 2, p. 9). At 26.4 m³/s, every kelvin of supply above the room carries about 32 kW, so a supply 6 K over the hall delivers about 190 kW of space heating.

**Coil loads.** The coil heats the fresh air (after heat recovery) to the hall's temperature and adds the fabric loss. In winter the outside air is dry, so the fresh air needed to carry away the evaporation is small: the Olympic's 0.045 kg/s of water against a moisture difference of about 12 g/kg (hall at 29 °C, 60 %, about 15 g/kg; outside at −3 °C saturated, about 3 g/kg) needs only about 3.6 kg/s (3 m³/s) of fresh air; normal design holds a minimum of about 30 % fresh air when occupied. A worked design-day estimate for the Olympic at −3 °C, 30 % fresh air (8 m³/s) through a plate recuperator of 55 % effectiveness: ventilation about 140 kW, fabric about 100 kW (the game's 3.06 kW/K × 32 K), **about 240 kW at minimum fresh air, rising to about 550 kW with 100 % fresh air** (summer purge mode or a dehumidification override in mild humid weather). The game's Olympic coils, 449 kW, sit inside that range and are a normal selection. Its loss conductance (10.3 kW/K, of which 7.2 kW/K is "infiltration") is a little higher than the estimate's 7.4 kW/K (fabric 3.1, net ventilation 4.3), because its 0.5 air changes of infiltration stand in for unrecovered ventilation: within the uncertainty, and on the safe side. On a −3 °C design day the game asks about 333 kW of its 449 kW, a headroom of 1.35. For the Lounge the same method gives about 50 kW at design and a coil of 65 to 80 kW; the game's 63 kW is at the low end.

Expressed per m² of hall floor, pool hall air heating is roughly **90 to 130 W/m²** at design with heat recovery, and twice that without it.

**Heat recovery.** The Carbon Trust lists plate heat exchangers, run-around coils (less effective, through pipe losses and pump energy) and thermal wheels, and heat-pump dehumidifiers, which recover the latent heat of the evaporation too (CTG009, pp. 11 to 12); a heat-pump maker claims about 300 % heat recovery to the pool water and air against 50 to 60 % for recuperator AHUs (Calorex, South Moorlands Leisure Centre). The Non-Domestic Building Services Compliance Guide's minimum dry heat recovery efficiencies are 50 % for a plate recuperator, 45 % for a run-around coil, 60 % for heat pipes and 65 % for a thermal wheel (from the 2013 edition; quoted from memory, so check before relying on them). For a 2000s centre the normal fit is a plate recuperator of about 50 to 65 %, or a thermal wheel of 65 to 75 %, with a bypass for summer.

## 5. Domestic hot water for the showers

**Temperatures.** Hot water is stored at 60 °C or above and distributed so it reaches 50 °C at outlets within a minute, against Legionella (HSE, HSG274 Part 2 and ACOP L8); showers are blended by thermostatic mixing valves to about 38 to 41 °C. So a calorifier needs its primary at 70 to 80 °C to hold 60 °C: if the ring sags much below about 70 °C, the DHW cannot reach its pasteurising temperature, which is a real consequence the game could show.

**Per shower and per bather.** A shower runs at about 6 to 10 l/min mixed (9 to 10 l/min allowed for a reasonable shower in a UK leisure specification, of which about 6 l/min hot); public pools use push-button showers that run 20 to 30 s a push (Hamworthy). A bather showering for about three minutes before and after a swim uses about **25 to 30 litres at 40 °C**, about 15 to 18 litres of 60 °C water blended with cold at 10 °C, about **0.9 to 1.0 kWh** at the tap. Hamworthy's worst case: one shower running for an hour dumps 328 litres of hot water (its 9 l/min at 60 % hot share). Sport England allows 30 litres of make-up water per swimmer for the pools, a separate figure (Sport England 2013, Appendix 2, p. 3).

**Daily and peak.** A centre with a 50 m and a leisure pool might see 1,000 to 2,000 swims a day; at about 1 kWh each that is 1 to 2 MWh a day at the taps, and with secondary circulation and storage losses (which in a long leisure-centre loop can be 30 to 50 % of the useful heat) **1.5 to 3 MWh a day, a mean of 60 to 120 kW**. The peak is the problem: sports facilities draw all their hot water at once (the end of a school session, a club's training, a gala), so storage is large (Hamworthy). Forty showers running together draw 40 × 9 l/min, about 750 kW instantaneous; over a 15-minute peak that is about 5,400 litres at 40 °C, about 3,200 litres at 60 °C. Normal practice is two storage calorifiers of about 1,500 to 2,500 litres each, with LTHW primary coils giving a recovery of **about 150 to 300 kW** in all, so that the store recovers within the hour between peaks. The Carbon Trust recommends that refurbishments use dedicated water heaters in place of storage calorifiers, which lose heat standing (CTG009, p. 17), but a 2000s centre on a boiler-house ring would have the calorifiers.

For the game's changing area, when it is built: a DHW consumer with a store (about 4,000 litres at 60 °C, 4,000 × 4.19 = 16,760 kJ/K), a recovery of up to 250 kW from the ring, and a draw following the bathers (about 1 kWh a bather, peaking with the sessions).

## 6. Museum space heating

**Load.** Design heat-loss figures per m² of floor depend on insulation, height and ventilation. A building-type table of design heat loads gives a museum about 61 W/m², an office about 89 W/m², a sports hall about 64 W/m² and a public pool about 80 W/m² (nPro, from German practice; indicative only). A well-insulated UK building of about 2000 (Part L 1995/2002: walls 0.35 to 0.45, roof 0.25, glazing about 2 to 3.3 W/m²K) comes out at about 50 to 70 W/m² at −3 °C outside and 20 °C inside; galleries are tall (4 to 6 m) and their visitors want fresh air, which pushes a museum towards the upper half. The game's 50 W/m² is at the low end; **60 W/m² is the better central figure**. CIBSE Guide A's winter design temperature for southern England is about −3 °C (the 99.6th percentile; not checked against the current edition), which the game already uses.

**Museum-specific conditions.** The National Museum Directors' Conference's guiding principles (2009, adopted by the Bizot Group): for hygroscopic materials, a stable relative humidity in the range 40 to 60 % and a stable temperature in the range 16 to 25 °C, with tighter control for sensitive objects such as panel paintings and wider for stone and ceramics; the IIC and ICOM-CC declaration of 2014 confirmed them. What matters is stability, not a precise figure: a museum's heating avoids fast swings, and in historic houses "conservation heating" (heating to keep RH below about 65 %, not for comfort) is common practice. So the museum's thermostat should hold its temperature with a slow, gentle control: a drop of a few degrees over a night is acceptable, a sudden swing is not. Its fabric is heavy: ISO 13790's internal heat capacities per m² of floor are 80 kJ/K (very light), 110 (light), 165 (medium), 260 (heavy) and 370 (very heavy); a masonry museum is medium to heavy, **about 200 kJ/K per m²**, twice the game's 100. With 3,038 m² at 200 kJ/K per m² (608 MJ/K) and a UA of about 7.9 kW/K (60 W/m² over 23 K), its time constant is about 21 hours: a cold night costs it a degree or two, and it needs a day to recover from a long cut, as such buildings do.

**Annual check.** CIBSE TM46's "cultural activities" benchmark (museum, art gallery or other public building) is 200 kWh/m² a year of fossil-thermal energy and 70 kWh/m² of electricity; CIBSE's newer dataset for museums and galleries has medians of 132 kWh/m² of gas and 86 kWh/m² of electricity. The game's museum at UA 7.9 kW/K over about 2,800 degree-days (base about 18 °C, held at 20 °C) uses about 530 MWh of heat a year, about 175 kWh/m², between the two.

## 7. Reception and offices

Design loads of about **50 to 70 W/m²** for offices of the period at 21 °C inside and −3 °C outside (nPro gives 89 W/m² for older stock); a leisure centre's reception and foyer has busy entrance doors and more glass, so **about 70 W/m² at 20 °C**. CIBSE TM46's general office benchmark is 120 kWh/m² a year of fossil-thermal and 95 of electricity. Sport England: ancillary areas 20 °C (Appendix 2, footnote 7, from HSE HSG179).

The changing area is a heavier load than its floor suggests: Sport England asks for 24 to 25 °C and 10 air changes an hour on its own system. At 3 m high that is about 8 l/s per m² of floor; heating it from −3 to 24 °C is about 270 W/m² with no recovery and **about 100 to 120 W/m²** with a 55 to 60 % recuperator, plus the DHW of section 5.

So a centre of this size needs about 1 MW on a design day, and the game's six boilers of 300 kW, 1,800 kW in all and 1,500 kW with one out, give N+1 with about 50 % margin for warm-ups, as normal practice does. Not every heater runs flat out at once: the full peak column is reached only when a pool is being warmed from cold on a winter day, which the operators would schedule. For comparison, five UK leisure centres converted to heat pumps had more than 5.5 MW of gas boilers between them, about 1.1 MW each (Co-operative Councils Innovation Network, West Northamptonshire and Telford & Wrekin), and those were mostly 25 m pool centres; a 50 m pool centre's 1.8 MW is in proportion.

**Typical boiler size.** Modular condensing boilers of the 2000s and later run from about 50 to 300 kW a module (Hamworthy's Wessex ModuMax range reached about 290 kW, Remeha's Gas 310/610 ECO from about 160 kW to over 1 MW); a 1.8 MW plant is commonly six 300 kW modules or three 600 kW floor-standing boilers, with a modulation of about 5:1 (20 % to 100 %) each. The game's six HW 300 boilers are a normal selection.

**Annual gas.** CIBSE TM46 (2008), the Display Energy Certificate benchmarks: a "swimming pool centre" (pool hall, changing and ancillaries) 1,130 kWh/m² a year of fossil-thermal energy and 245 kWh/m² of electricity, per m² of gross internal area (the TM46 values were confirmed in the TM46 PDF and on GreenCalculus; the earlier ECG078 (2001) pool benchmarks were not found online). The Welsh Government: "a larger leisure centre with a pool requires on average more than two million kWh of natural gas and more than half a million kWh of electricity per year" (2023). Sport England's typical energy profile for a pool building: space heating 53 %, water heating 25 %, fans and pumps 10 %, lighting 6.5 %, general power 5.5 %.

The game's mean loads (at 12 °C, its `AMBIENT`) are about 145 kW for the pools' water, 210 kW for the halls' air, 70 to 90 kW for DHW when built, and about 60 kW for the museum averaged over the year: about 500 kW, **about 4.4 GWh of heat a year**, about 4.8 GWh of gas at a 90 % seasonal efficiency, of which the wet side (pools, halls and DHW) about 4.2 GWh. With a wet-side gross internal area of about 5,000 to 6,000 m² (the two pool halls 3,200 m², changing, plant, reception and circulation), TM46 gives 5.7 to 6.8 GWh, so the game's centre sits at about 60 to 75 % of the typical stock: a reasonably efficient centre with heat recovery, as a 2000s building should be. The share is plausible too: space heating (the halls' air plus the museum) about 55 %, water heating (pools and DHW) about 45 %.

On the Olympic's heater: 750 kW is normal practice, but it more than doubles the largest single draw on the ring, and with every heater at full output the sum (about 1,950 kW when everything is built) slightly exceeds the 1,800 kW supply. That is realistic (a cold refill on a winter day makes the ring sag, and operators avoid it), and it is what mission 120's "demand above supply" test wants. If the owner prefers the game's slower warm-up, 450 kW (0.15 K/h, about five days from cold) is a defensible middle; the game's 293 kW is slow but not wrong. Either way the pool's thermostat governs it: the warm-up headroom is used only when the water is well below its set point.

On the outside temperature: size every consumer at one design outside temperature, −3 °C (CIBSE Guide A, southern England), and run them at the world's ambient. Today the pools are sized at 12 °C (`AMBIENT`), so their "design loads" are mean loads and their headroom is consumed by the winter: give `thermalParams` its loads at −3 °C, or keep 12 °C and accept that the effective headroom on a design day is about 1.35 for the Olympic's air.

**Checks the game's figures should pass** (each a test or an assertion):

1. A new game: the ring at 80 °C, supply equal to the consumers' draw plus about 18 kW of losses, every pool at its set point (mission 120's new-game-normal test).
2. Design day (ambient −3 °C): the built consumers' steady demand is about 650 to 750 kW today and at most about 1,050 kW with everything built, below the N+1 capacity of 1,500 kW; every consumer's `k × (80 − T_secondary)` at its set point is at least its design-day demand, so with the ring hot nothing goes short.
3. Ring at design load: the return is about 60 °C at full output (1,800 kW at 80 m³/h is a 19 K drop) and about 72 °C at a 700 kW load.
4. Gas cut: with about 700 kW drawn and nothing supplied the ring falls about 2 K a minute (80 to 50 °C in about 15 minutes), as mission 120 asks ("over minutes").
5. No heat at all: the Olympic cools about 1 K a day, the Lounge about 3 to 4 K a day, the jacuzzi about 0.5 K an hour, the museum a degree or two overnight.
6. Warm-up from a cold fill at 10 °C: the Olympic no faster than 0.25 K/h (three to four days at 750 kW; nine to ten days at 293 kW), the Lounge about 0.3 to 0.5 K/h, the jacuzzi to 37 °C within about eight hours.
7. Annual energy over a simulated year at the UK's mean ambient: about 4 to 5 GWh of heat (4.5 to 5.5 GWh of gas), the wet side within about 60 to 100 % of TM46's 1,130 kWh/m² over its gross area, the museum within about 130 to 200 kWh/m².
8. Evaporation: the Olympic's 162 kg/h equals ASHRAE's formula at an activity factor of 0.65; any change to its humidity or temperatures should keep it within the 0.5 to 1.0 band.
9. DHW, when built: the store held at 60 °C or above while the ring is at 80 °C; with the ring below about 70 °C it cannot reach 60 °C, and the game should flag it.
